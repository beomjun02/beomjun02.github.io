Related work & method

We model manipulation as the evolution of a single labeled 3D point set that contains the scene and the embodiment together. A language-conditioned flow-matching transformer predicts the displacement of every point over a short horizon; because the hand's points are part of the prediction, generation is the action, and joint commands follow by per-link Procrustes and inverse kinematics. There is no separate world model, no policy head, no retargeting network and no inverse-dynamics model. On top of this we add supervision on the one structure a per-point generative objective cannot express — which part of the hand is touching which part of which object — stated in a part vocabulary that human and robot hands share.

Related work, and what is actually ours

Predicting future 3D point motion from human video, and using it to drive a robot, is an active and crowded line of work. We read the nine closest systems in detail. The comparison below is not a ranking — several of them report real-robot success rates we do not yet have — but an attempt to say precisely which axis each one occupies, because on one axis the field is unanimous and we are the exception.

The prediction targets: object flow, and who owns it

General Flow is the strongest evidence that this family works: a language-conditioned 3D flow model trained on RGBD human video reaches 81 % zero-shot human-to-robot success across 18 tasks in 6 scenes with no in-domain fine-tuning, on rigid, articulated and soft objects. Its flow is the future trajectory of points on objects of interest — the hand is not part of the prediction, so the flow is guidance that a separate closed-loop policy must consume. PointWorld scales the same idea to a 1B-parameter point transformer over ~2M trajectories, and its own ablation finds that gripper point flows beat end-effector poses and joint positions as an action parameterisation. But its robot points are an input: the model is asked what the scene does given a gripper trajectory. Its stated limitations are candid and, for our purposes, diagnostic — a static-initial-state assumption, no physics priors, drift under autoregressive rollout, and "correlation vs causation," which is precisely the failure we measure as objects floating toward a hand that has not closed.

The embodiment: everyone abstracts it away

This is the axis on which the field is unanimous. EgoWAM normalises human and robot into a shared 14-dimensional end-effector space before training, and concludes that "our gains stay at the context level; learning novel motion primitives from human data remains out of reach." HumanEgo — the strongest real-world result in the group, 92.5 % across four tasks from 30 minutes of human video each — achieves it by treating the human hand as a parallel-jaw gripper, thumb and index only, and represents objects as 6-DoF poses rather than points. ContactFlow reduces the hand to where force is applied, and extracts that with off-the-shelf HaMeR and HACO at both training and inference, noting that HACO "is not reliable in predicting whether the hand makes contact at all." Each abstraction is a reasonable engineering choice and each buys real robustness. Each also makes finger-level dexterity unrepresentable by construction.

Our state contains the embodiment at part granularity and predicts it jointly with the scene, so generation is the action and no separate policy, retargeter or inverse-dynamics model stands between the prediction and the robot. That is the claim this paper has to earn, and it is the one the table shows is unoccupied.

Contact: prior art we do not contest

Our contact supervision is not novel and we do not present it as such. ContactGen already predicts, per object point, a contact probability, a hand part map over B = 16 MANO parts, and a direction map. ManiDext replaces the discrete part label with continuous geodesic correspondence embeddings over the 778 MANO vertices and reports it better on every metric, arguing discrete labels "fall short in providing semantic information." Both are grasp synthesis: ContactGen static, ManiDext conditioned on a given object trajectory. Neither predicts scene dynamics, and neither is used to make a world model's rollout better.

What is unoccupied is the placement: contact identity as a per-frame auxiliary inside a generative model of total scene flow. iMaC is the nearest attempt and uses contact as a control input rather than a prediction, reporting a modest gain (FID 38.81 → 36.96, FVD 523.94 → 489.51). We therefore treat contact supervision as a component with citable prior art and a small expected effect, not as a contribution — and we report it against a control that has it switched off.

Two findings from this literature that changed our experiments

Auxiliaries that work here are kinematic, not contact. HumanEgo's ablation attributes +17.5 points to its object-motion objective, +12.5 to latent consistency and +5 to a 2D trace, +25 combined — and not one of its three auxiliaries involves contact. That is the opposite of our campaign's bet, so rather than argue we imported the object-motion objective into our framework as a controlled arm (§5). It is non-trivial here precisely because our state is dense points: an object's rigid motion is nowhere in our output space, so the flow head can satisfy its per-point loss without any token representing that the object rotated.

Our contact labels sit in the regime where plain cross-entropy fails. HACO measures non-contact to contact ratios of 2.7:1 on DexYCB, 19.5:1 on InterHand2.6M and 21.7:1 on Decaf, and shows balanced sampling and a vertex-level class-balanced loss beating focal, CB and LDAM (+12.0 recall, +8.5 F1 on MOW). We measured 3.97 % contacting points in our corpus — about 24:1 — and confirmed the predicted failure directly: an unbalanced run reached 97.4 % accuracy with exactly zero accuracy on contacting points. Our contact arms use a class-balanced objective for this reason.

Where we are behind, stated plainly

General Flow reports 81 % zero-shot real-robot success and HumanEgo 92.5 %; our closed-loop GR-1 result is 0 of 3, with the hand stalling 15–20 cm short. PointWorld trains a 1B-parameter model on ~2M trajectories; our fast-iteration rig is 39M parameters on 5,645 episodes. Those gaps are real and are not closed by any argument about representation. What the representation buys has to show up as dexterous behaviour those systems cannot express — bimanual, finger-level, contact-rich — and demonstrating that, rather than matching a pick-and-place success rate, is the experiment this work owes.

3.1

Scene and embodiment as one labeled point set

A state is a set of points carrying semantic labels. At frame t,

St = { (xi, ℓi) }i=1…P,   xi ∈ ℝ³,   ℓi = (class, instance, part, side, tag)

class separates hand from object. instance identifies the entity a point belongs to, so two objects are distinguishable without any ordering convention. part is the semantic sub-unit of an entity — for hands, the sixteen MANO parts. side is left or right, tag carries the per-entity language phrase. Point identity is stable across the frames of a window: point i at frame t and frame t+1 are the same material point, which is what makes displacement a well-defined per-point quantity rather than a set-matching problem.

Each entity is sampled to a fixed budget of 512 points, hands and objects alike, and a window admits at most two hands and six objects. Uniform density matters because contact is a geometric measurement: at tabletop scale, 512 points on an object is roughly 1 cm spacing, and coarser sampling quantises the very quantity the model is asked to reason about.

The consequence of putting the embodiment inside the state is that there is no action space distinct from the observation space. A policy, in this formulation, is a generative model of how the world — hand included — continues.

3.2

Language-conditioned scene flow

Given the frame-0 points, their labels and a natural-language instruction c, the model predicts the displacement of every point over the next T frames:

Δ = fθ(x0, ℓ, c) ∈ ℝT×P×3,   xt = x0 + Δt
T = 10 frames at 10 Hz — a one-second chunk

Predicting total scene flow, rather than object motion conditioned on a given hand trajectory, is deliberate: the hand and the objects it acts on are generated jointly by one distribution, so the model is never given the answer to half of the interaction. Longer horizons come from chaining — each chunk is re-conditioned on the model's own final predicted state — which is also the regime in which the model is deployed on a robot, where the controller replans every few executed frames.

3.3

Tokenization and backbone

Part-major patches

Attending over individual points is quadratic in P and wasteful, since neighbouring points on a rigid part move almost identically. We group points into patches of k = 8, ordered part-major: points are sorted first by entity and part, then along a Hilbert curve within the part, and consecutive runs of eight become one token. A token is therefore a coherent two-centimetre piece of one semantic part of one entity — never a mixture of a thumb and the mug it holds. This is what later allows contact to be expressed between tokens rather than between raw points, and it is why the part label, not merely the instance label, is load-bearing.

Backbone

The T × G patch tokens are processed by a DiT with full three-dimensional attention — every token attends every other token at every frame, with no factorisation into spatial and temporal passes. Rotary position encoding is applied on the time axis only; spatial position enters through the point coordinates themselves rather than through an index, so the model is not committed to any canonical ordering of entities or points. Blocks are adaLN-zero conditioned on the diffusion time, with RMSNorm on queries and keys and a SwiGLU feed-forward. The instruction is encoded by a frozen T5 and injected per entity through the tag label, so a phrase attaches to the object it names.

Two design choices were tested and rejected, and we report them as such: factorised or entity-then-scene attention, and auxiliary reconstruction losses. Neither improved on the plain configuration.

Flow matching

Training uses conditional flow matching with v-parameterisation. With τ ∈ [0,1] the noise level, Δ1 the ground-truth flow and ε ~ 𝒩(0, I),

Δτ = (1 − τ)·ε + τ·Δ1
ℒfm = 𝔼τ,ε ‖ vθ(Δτ, τ, x0, ℓ, c) − (Δ1 − ε) ‖²  masked to existing, valid points

Sampling integrates the learned field with sixteen Euler steps. An exponential moving average of the weights (decay 0.999) is used for all evaluation.

3.4

Contact correspondencemeasured · §4

The objective is marginal; the phenomenon is relational

The flow-matching loss decomposes into a sum of per-point terms. Its minimiser therefore depends only on the marginal conditional distribution of each point's displacement given the scene. But an object accelerates because a particular part of a hand is pressing on a particular part of its surface: contact is a property of a pair of points, and no quantity of per-point supervision identifies it. A model can drive ℒfm down by learning the correlation hand near object ⇒ object moves without ever representing the mechanism that licenses it.

That shortcut predicts specific failures under distribution shift, and we observe all three: objects translate toward a hand that has not closed on them; both hands are recruited when only the nearer one is acting; and grasps form whose contacts do not oppose one another. We therefore supervise the relation directly, as a relation.

An assignment between patch tokens

Let H be the hand patch tokens and O the object patch tokens at a given frame, taken from the same final-layer activations the flow head reads. We project both into a small matching space and score every pair by inner product:

qh = Wq zh,   ko = Wk zo   (dm = 128)
Sho = ⟨qh, ko⟩ / √dm
Sh· ← [ nh , Sh1, …, ShO ]  nh = learned no-contact logit

A softmax over each hand row — object patches plus the no-contact slot — gives a distribution over what this part of the hand is touching, if anything. The same matrix, transposed and given its own no-contact slot, is normalised down each object column to give what is touching this part of the object, if anything. Sharing one compatibility matrix between the two directions is both cheaper and stricter than learning two: a single notion of "these two patches are in contact" must satisfy both normalisations.

Targets are built from ground-truth point-level correspondences, coarsened to patches and left soft: for each hand patch we count how many of its eight points contact each object patch and normalise. A grasp that straddles two adjacent object patches puts mass on both rather than forcing an arbitrary winner — appropriate, since adjacent patches are about a centimetre apart. Rows with no contact place all mass on the no-contact slot. The loss is the soft cross-entropy in both directions, averaged over scorable rows and columns.

Relation to contrastive representation learning

Row-wise, the loss is InfoNCE: a softmax over inner products, pulling a hand patch toward the object patch it touches and pushing it from the rest, with the no-contact slot as an explicit negative for the common case of touching nothing. The natural simplification — raise the cosine similarity of contacting tokens directly, with no projection — is worse for a specific reason. The tokens are read by a linear flow head, so their geometry already encodes predicted displacement, and forcing contacting hand and object tokens together in that space is forcing them to similar predicted motion. That is right for a rigid grasp and wrong for a hand resting on a stationary object, where contact is total and relative motion is zero; more importantly it rewards exactly the co-motion shortcut we are trying to remove. Projecting into a separate matching space first — the standard reason contrastive objectives use a projection head — keeps the contact geometry off the feature the flow head reads. The projection is two matrices, and like the rest of the component it is discarded at inference.

Why an assignment rather than a regressed contact point

Contact is discrete, combinatorial and set-valued under uncertainty. Regressing a contact coordinate imposes a metric on a multimodal quantity, so when two objects are plausible partners the squared-error optimum is the mean of the modes — a location in contact with nothing. A coordinate also has to be expressed in some frame: in the object's own frame it is not decodable without separately naming the object, and the frame must itself be estimated, which confounds "wrong place on the object" with "wrong idea of how the object is turned". An assignment has none of these problems. Predicting an object patch names its object by construction, the output space matches the phenomenon, and ambiguity appears as a spread distribution rather than a meaningless average.

Why this is the quantity that transfers

Patches are part-major, and both robot hands we deploy on map onto the same sixteen-part MANO taxonomy that labels the human demonstrations — the Sharpa hand through its URDF links, the GR-1 Fourier hand through a link-to-part table that splits fused segments along their long axis. A correspondence of the form index fingertip ↔ this patch of the mug is therefore stated in a vocabulary shared by the demonstrator and the robot, whereas joint angles, link poses and absolute hand-point positions are not. Our central claim is that a model trained only on human demonstrations executes on a robot without adaptation; this component is the proposed mechanism for that transfer rather than an empirical accident, and §4 tests it as such.

The head is discarded at inference: the model is evaluated on rollout accuracy and interaction diagnostics, not on its own matching accuracy. The prediction we pre-register is specific — if the account above is right, the benefit should concentrate in the interaction measures rather than spread uniformly over end-point error, should grow with scene crowding, where the correlational shortcut is most ambiguous, and should survive the change of embodiment. A capacity or regularisation explanation predicts none of these.

Outcome (2026-09-08). The first prediction holds: E2's gain is concentrated in the interaction measures — object motion without contact falls from 0.583 to 0.484, the largest reduction of any arm — while its end-point error moves only 0.40 cm. The crowding and cross-embodiment predictions are not yet tested. Full table and caveats in §4.

3.5

From flow to joint commands

Because the embodiment's points are predicted alongside the scene, no learned decoder is needed to turn a prediction into a command. Points carry a part label, and a part corresponds to a rigid link. For each link we take the predicted positions of its points at frame t against their frame-0 positions and solve the orthogonal Procrustes problem for the rigid transform that best explains them:

Rℓ, tℓ = arg minR ∈ SO(3), t Σi ∈ ℓ ‖ R xi0 + t − x̂it ‖²

The resulting per-link poses are the target of an inverse-kinematics solve against the robot's URDF, which yields joint angles. The chain is deterministic and carries no learned parameters, so nothing about it is fitted to a particular embodiment: swapping robots means swapping a URDF and a link-to-part table. Errors are attributable — a failure is a bad predicted point cloud or an infeasible pose, and the two are separable.

On the robot the model runs closed-loop: a chunk is predicted, a few frames are executed, and the next chunk is conditioned on the resulting observation. The model is therefore deployed on its own distribution of conditioning states, which is why the autoregressive rollout, not the single teacher-forced chunk, is our primary evaluation.

3.6

Data and training

Corpus

Six human hand-object capture datasets — TACO, ARCTIC, DexYCB, OakInk2, GRAB and HOT3D — are converted to the common labeled-point representation at 10 Hz, with per-frame validity and point-level contact annotations. Robot-embodiment episodes come from retargeting a subset to the Sharpa hand, which lets us measure cross-embodiment behaviour directly rather than only through simulation.

Window trimming

Raw episodes begin and end with the demonstrator moving to and from a ready pose — motion that is real but carries no interaction. Training and evaluation windows are trimmed to the interaction interval of each take, which retains 89.9 % of candidate windows. Windows are additionally filtered for tracking jumps, parked segments and excessive invalidity.

Augmentation

The scene origin is jittered by up to one metre per sample. This is not cosmetic: it prevents the model from reading absolute position as a task cue, and it is why any auxiliary target must be equivariant to a global translation.

Contact buys interaction, data buys accuracy, structure costs both

The two contact arms are the only ones that improve interaction and accuracy together, and E2 gives the largest reduction in object-motion-without-contact — 0.484 against the control's 0.583. Its head is not decorative: accuracy in naming which entity a contacting point touches rises from 5.7 % to 71.3 %, hand-part accuracy from 60.8 % to 84.3 %, and accuracy on contact-onset frames — the rare, hard ones — from 6.0 % to 78.6 %. Cross-embodiment co-training gives the best accuracy, consistent with our earlier finding that it behaves like more data rather than a new domain. Both structural additions cost accuracy.

The result that bears on the literature

HumanEgo attributes +17.5 points to its object-motion auxiliary, the largest of its three, and reports no contact term at all. We imported that objective and it is null to slightly negative here (−0.20 cm). The likely reason is representational: HumanEgo's state is a 6-DoF pose token, so predicting object motion reinforces its native variable, whereas our state is a dense point set in which rigid motion is a derived quantity the flow head never needs. A controlled comparison of contact against kinematic auxiliaries on one rig is not something the cited work provides, and the answer differs by representation.

What this does not show

The matched basis understates the contact arms: E1 is +0.29 cm with OMWC 0.552 at 30,000 steps, but +1.23 cm with OMWC 0.399 at 50,000 on its own basis — effects grow with training, as they have throughout this project. The closed loop did not move: GR-1 zero-shot for E1 is 0 of 3 with a median final hand–object distance of 19.67 cm, unchanged, because no arm addressed reach, which remains the binding failure. And E3's arm is not repairable as run — two-stream is 38 % slower per step, so under a fixed hour budget it cannot be step-matched, which is itself a cost the structural-prior literature does not report.
