Experiment log · Dexterous Point Policy

Four poses cannot estimate a success rate

The sim benchmark placed the object at the exact centre of its grid cell in every episode, so a 512-episode run was four initial conditions repeated 128 times each. Turning on 1 cm of per-episode position jitter moves the aggregate by 0.3 points and changes what every number on this benchmark means. Re-measured: the shipped baseline, and the cross-hand table that the embodiment argument rests on.

Verdict

The old protocol was unbiased in aggregate and degenerate per cell. Averaged over bottle, box and ball the shipped checkpoint scores 22.9 % under 1 cm jitter against 23.2 % at the exact cell centres — a 0.3-point difference. But individual cells had been sitting pinned at 0/128 and 128/128, which means the cell was reporting the outcome of the single pose it contained rather than the policy's competence. The clearest consequence is the tennis ball: 1.4 % → 6.4 %, a 4.6× error, because the exact-centre pose happens to be atypically hard for it.

The cross-hand result survives the change and gets stronger. At 96 episodes per hand the intervals no longer overlap — Inspire is at least 17.4 %, while Wuji and Sharpa are at most 3.85 % — and the failure mechanism reproduces the previous study to within a millimetre of IK residual under completely different initial conditions. Changing every starting pose changed the success numbers slightly and changed the reason for failure not at all.

And on the corrected benchmark the contact-label question finally has an answer — the opposite one. At a matched 370 k steps on 512 identical initial conditions per object, the human annotation beats the learned tactile label on bottle (−8.0 points, p = 0.031) and box (−10.9, p = 5e−05), losing only on ball (+2.5) where neither policy is competent. The 07-30 log read the other way because it compared auto@80 k against oracle@60 k with both checkpoints picked on bottle alone. Since that human label is one grasp bit copied across five fingers, the finding is not "human labels are better" but something more awkward: a 1-bit label trains a better policy here than a genuine 5-bit one.

01The defect

The eval driver built its InitialStateTable with xy_jitter_m=0.0. The jitter machinery existed — it had simply never been switched on.

jitter 0 (until today)jitter 1 cm (from today)
distinct initial conditions per 512-episode run4512

valid intervalcell-level mean ± 1.96 SE (n=4)Wilson binomial (n=512)

The within-cell divergence was real — final object positions differ by 16–30 cm inside one cell — but it is simulator noise, not task variation. Every binomial interval this harness has ever produced was therefore describing sensitivity to PhysX at one fixed pose. The point estimates were always valid as descriptions of what happened at four poses.

04Does one centimetre decide the outcome?

A question the old benchmark could not express. Within one cell, split the 128 episodes at the median offset and compare. exploratory

object · cell · axisbelow medianabove medianΔ
box · cell 3 · dx96.9 %66.7 %−30.3
bottle · cell 0 · dx13.8 %41.3 %+27.4
bottle · cell 3 · dx53.8 %34.9 %−18.9
ball · cell 1 · dy9.2 %23.8 %+14.6

Individual cells are strongly offset-dependent — but the sign is cell-specific, so it does not aggregate. Pooled within-cell effects mostly straddle zero; only 2 of 6 exclude it, and with six uncorrected tests that is roughly what chance produces. Distance from the anchor separates nothing on any object. This is a post-hoc split of data collected for another purpose and it supplies no headline claim.

The useful reading is that at the centimetre scale success is strongly pose-dependent but the dependence is local, not monotone — which argues for sampling poses more strongly than a clean gradient would have. A smooth response could have been handled by four poses plus an offset model; a lumpy one can only be sampled.

A count of zero is a floor, not a point estimate, so the claim lives entirely in the upper bound — and that bound depends on n. At the previous study's n=24 a zero only supported "at most 13.8 %"; at n=96 it supports "at most 3.85 %" (Wilson two-sided; Clopper–Pearson gives 3.77 % two-sided, 3.07 % one-sided). That is why the episode count was quadrupled. The intervals are now disjoint — 17.4 % floor against a 3.85 % ceiling — so the gap is resolved by the data rather than asserted.

06What was fixed in advance

The previous log selected both checkpoints on bottle alone and the per-object table then reversed the ranking. That freedom is removed by pre-registration, written before any eval on the new protocol was submitted.

rule, fixed before the fact
checkpointthe 400 k snapshot of each variant, chosen by step count alone. If training falls short, the largest step both reached — read off the filesystem by a script that prints "selection used snapshot availability only".
A1 · launch cutoffprimary evals must launch by 01:30 at whatever the largest common snapshot is then. Adds a deadline, does not touch the selection rule.
A2 · training continuationchained afterany chunks permitted — the selection rule is invariant to how long training runs.
A3 · paired comparisonboth variants run the same 512 initial conditions, so the primary is the paired difference with McNemar's exact test on discordant pairs. Independent binomials demoted to secondary.
null resulta CI covering zero is reported as "no detectable difference at this sample size", with the width stated — not as equivalence, and not resolved by per-object sign patterns.

A3 exists because of section 04: on a locally lumpy response surface a few points of difference between two variants could be pose noise, and pairing differences the pose out at no extra cost. The pairing is verified, not assumed — the analysis script refuses to run unless both runs agree on jitter, episode count, the exact episode-id set and a recomputed initial-condition hash.

Annotation added 2026-08-01 — these rates were measured through a degraded observation

A forensic replay found that the object point cloud the policy is fed at deploy does not match the one it was trained on. Measured by rotation-invariant PCA extent, over 24 bottle frames each:

training h5   100 % distinct points  ·  extent 7.0 × 2.6 × 1.7 cm
sim / deploy   24.4 % distinct  ·  extent 5.9 × 0.9 × 0.5 cm

The longest axis roughly matches (0.84×) but the second and third are 2.9× and 3.4× thinner: the policy sees a flat ribbon where it learned a three-dimensional shell. And 76 % of the 2048-point tensor is one point repeated — the padding rule filling slots that erosion emptied. Registration is correct; the points do land on the bottle.

Cause: the harness still applies 15 px image-space ring erosion to the bottle, while the training pipeline was rebuilt in July with depth-gradient filtering because erosion was destroying objects — the same defect that had rendered a 6.7 cm tennis ball as a 1.2 cm speck. The training data was fixed; the simulator's observation was not.

What this does and does not affect. The absolute success rates on this page — here and in section 03 — were all measured through that degraded observation and should be read as such. The paired comparison below is internally valid: both variants were scored through the identical degraded pipeline on identical initial conditions, so the difference between them is not explained by it. An erosion sweep against the training band is running; any re-measurement will be published as its own result, not folded into this table.

The human label wins, and that is the awkward result. The annotation it wins with is one grasp bit copied across five fingers (1.21 distinct finger columns of 5 over 69 k frames, section 03 of the previous log), while the learned tactile label is a genuine 5-bit signal at 4.98/5. So a strictly less informative label trains a better policy on two of three objects. The 07-30 log reported the reverse ranking; it compared checkpoints at different training steps (auto 80 k vs oracle 60 k), both selected on bottle alone. Matched and paired, the ordering flips.

Why the pooled row is demoted, not featured. Pooling 1536 episodes treats three tasks as exchangeable draws; they are not, and here they disagree about the sign — ball significantly favours the opposite side. The analysis emits a direction-conflict warning for exactly this case, so no single pooled figure is quoted. Ball should carry no weight in either direction regardless: episodes solved by both policies number 0 of 512, so its handful of successes are disjoint — the signature of near-chance, not of competence.

Why 370 k and not the pre-registered 400 k. At 00:40 the shared checkpoint volume went hard-full (a 4 KB write failed while df reported 1 % of a bogus 2.0 E size) and both continuation jobs died on filesystem writes — auto at step 387 100, oracle before training resumed. The pre-registered fallback took the largest step both reached, computed from the filesystem by a script that prints "selection used snapshot availability only — no eval result was consulted". auto is therefore scored at 370 k, not at its own 385 k: comparing auto@385 k with oracle@370 k would confound the label with 15 k extra steps. Matched beats maximal.

What is not a controlled comparison. Either variant against the shipped baseline. That row is 410 k steps at 128 points on the old data; these are 370 k at 2048 points on rebuilt data, so label, point count, training length and pipeline all differ at once — the same shape of confound as the original submission's +71.3-point claim. The baseline appears here as context, and no attribution is drawn from it.

Pairing was verified, not assumed: the analysis refuses to emit a number unless both runs agree on jitter, episode count, the exact episode-id set and a recomputed initial-condition hash — ball's is 82fbeaa7dc05e3f7; all three are recorded in results/PAIRED.md. Checked by deliberately breaking it — corrupted jitter and a truncated episode set each exit non-zero.
