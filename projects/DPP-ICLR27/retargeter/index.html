Distilling Functional Transfer into a Reusable Hand Retargeter

Working note · v1, 2026-08-08 METHOD FROZEN · RESULTS PENDING

ABSTRACT Existing human→robot hand retargeting is either pose-based — cheap, reusable, but blind to the object (kinematic matching demonstrably produces the worst grasps) — or object-based (functional) — correct, but locked inside per-demo/per-task RL policies that are never reusable. We distill the latter into the former's form: a small per-hand retargeter R (human hand keypoints → robot joint commands, closed-loop, per-step) and its inverse R⁻¹ (robot joints → human-equivalent hand), trained purely supervised on time-aligned human↔robot pairs mined from ManipTrans' released functional transfers (DexManipNet: 3.3K episodes, 1.2K objects, 1.34M frames), with no robot demonstrations and no task data. The load-bearing experiment holds architecture fixed and varies only the pairing criterion: the same R trained on kinematic pairs versus functional pairs. Results: pending (runs in progress).

1  Motivation

Retargeting decides whether human hand data is usable by a robot hand at all. Two failure modes motivate this work. First, pose-based retargeting ignores the object: in our own cross-hand study, a policy that scored 25% with the Inspire hand scored 0/96 with Wuji and Sharpa even after IK repair, and HRDexDB independently reports kinematic-retargeting initialization below a no-prior baseline (42.4% vs 69.7% episode success). Second, functional approaches don't produce a reusable module: DexMachina trains one policy per demonstration, ManipTrans retrains a residual per task, DexTrack's controller consumes robot-kinematic references — none yields a component that can be composed, unchanged, with a policy that emits human hand actions.

Our claim: the functional objective can be amortized into the reusable form — distill sim-verified functional transfers into a per-hand module trained once, reused across tasks. The retargeter is task-agnostic by construction: no task data from our downstream benchmarks enters its training; fine-tuning happens (elsewhere) only in the base policy, while R stays frozen. See Related Works for the full taxonomy; the empty cell it exposes — learned, closed-loop, per-hand retargeter trained from object-motion-matched pairs without robot demos or task data — is what this note fills.

2  Method

2.1  Interfaces

The human hand is represented as 21 keypoints: a wrist pose w ∈ SE(3) plus 20 wrist-relative joint positions k ∈ ℝ⁶⁰. Keypoints (rather than MANO θ) are the interface because every asset in play speaks points: the pretrained point-track policy trunk, dex-retargeting's native input, ManipTrans' 21-keypoint correspondence, and EgoDex's ARKit joints. The robot hand is its independent-joint vector q (Inspire: 6; the 6 coupled joints follow mechanically).

2.2  The retargeter pair R, R⁻¹

R   (per control tick):  [ k̂_k ∈ ℝ⁶⁰,  q_k ∈ ℝ⁶,  u_{k−1} ∈ ℝ⁶ ]  →  [ u_k = g_E(k̂_k) + Δq_k,   Δwrist_k ∈ ℝ⁶ ]
R⁻¹ (per obs tick):      [ q_k ∈ ℝ⁶ ]                            →  [ k̃_k ∈ ℝ⁶⁰ (via θ̃→FK),  Δ̃wrist_k ∈ ℝ⁶ ]
  • Residual over a geometric prior. g_E is off-the-shelf kinematic retargeting (dex-retargeting / AnyTeleop, called directly — it is teleop-grade real-time; no distilled surrogate). R learns only the correction Δq; λ‖Δ‖² regularization makes λ→∞ recover the pose-based baseline exactly, so the λ sweep traces the converter↔policy continuum.
  • Conditioning makes it a policy, not a converter. q_k is the measured state (contact stalls appear here), u_{k−1} the previous command (continuity; the commanded-vs-achieved gap is itself a contact/lag signal). Proprioceptive conditioning — not lookahead — is what separates the tracker class from the converter class in prior work.
  • Wrist as correspondence, not pinning. Robot wrist = T_static(ŵ) ⊕ Δwrist: a per-hand reach-scale calibration (initialized from our validated fingertip-vector alignment fit) plus a learned, bounded correction. R⁻¹ learns the inverse map; a round-trip consistency loss ties the two. The human-side wrist is a canonical representation — loop consistency, not physical coincidence, is the requirement.
  • R⁻¹ is an encoder, not a mirror-policy. Its contract is consistency over accuracy: report a plausible, temporally coherent human hand for the robot's state. Internally it predicts MANO θ̃ and emits FK(θ̃, βcanon) keypoints, guaranteeing anatomically valid output; an EMA filter enforces coherence. Input is q only — no conditioning, by design.
  • No object input anywhere. The object enters the objective (pair construction and evaluation), never the observation; R needs no camera at deployment.

2.3  Pair construction from DexManipNet

ManipTrans transfers human demonstrations (OakInk-V2, FAVOR) to robot hands via a generalist imitator plus per-task residual RL, releasing the successful transfers as DexManipNet — 3.3K episodes over 1.2K objects and 61 tasks (1.34M frames, Inspire hand). Because the residual tracking follows the source clock, each episode is a time-aligned functional pair by construction: per frame,

( k_t, w^H_t │ q_t, u_t, w^R_t │ object pose_t │ task/object metadata )

We parse these tuples directly; the 61 per-task RL trainings that produced them are a cost we never pay. The kinematic comparison set applies g_E frame-by-frame to the same source human streams — identical sources, objects and trajectories; only the pairing criterion differs. This makes the central ablation exactly controlled.

Hand-identity audit. ManipTrans' Inspire URDF and ours (dex-urdf lineage) agree on all 12 joint origins to 0.00 mm; they differ in tip-site placement (2.0–4.6 mm), joint limits (e.g. proximal 1.7 vs 1.47 rad), and mimic-coupling ratios (1.0/1.6/2.4 vs 1.064/1.334/0.667) — three teams' approximations of the same RH56 mechanism. We therefore define robot labels in the 6 independent joints, whose geometry is identical across conventions, and gate ingestion on (i) the fraction of frames exceeding our joint limits and (ii) the fingertip-FK discrepancy across coupling conventions over the dataset's q-distribution.

3.2  Data splits & gates

Pre-registered held-out splits along two axes: unseen task and unseen object. Ingestion gates (before any training): per-episode hand tag; command-vs-state label semantics; root-pose presence; object-pose presence; join-alignment spot check on sampled episodes; joint-limit-exceed fraction; coupling-FK discrepancy (§2.3). All hyperparameter selection uses the validation split only.

3.3  Ablations

  • Loss terms (on/off): −λ_Δ, −FK, −rate, −Δwrist (4 runs).
  • Weights (log grid, validation-selected): λ_Δ ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.1, 1} × wFK ∈ {0, 0.1, 1} (12 runs). The λ_Δ sweep doubles as the converter↔policy interpolation; the optimal-λ ‖Δ‖ distribution quantifies, in degrees, how much correction kinematic retargeting actually needs.
  • Capacity: {0.1, 1, 5, 50}M parameters on R-main (4 runs).

3.4  Metrics

Level 1 — pair space (held-out episodes, no simulator): joint MAE (deg), fingertip FK error (mm), Δwrist error (mm/deg); for R⁻¹: keypoint error (mm), frame-to-frame jitter, wrist round-trip error.

Level 2 — functional replay (ManipTrans' IsaacGym environment — the same simulator that produced the data, eliminating the sim-gap confound): drive each arm with held-out source human streams and measure whether the object reproduces its recorded trajectory — dense-ε pose-error profile, success at ε ∈ {1, 3} cm, lift/hold predicate — plus a perturbation probe (randomized mid-rollout wrenches; recovery rate) that a converter structurally cannot pass.

3.5  Pre-registered hypotheses

  1. H1 (central): R-main > R-kin on both levels; the Level-2 success gap is the paper's claim that functional pairing beats kinematic pairing.
  2. H2: R-main > g_E — learning adds value over optimization alone.
  3. H3: R-main > R-chunk, concentrated in grasp-acquisition segments and under perturbation — per-step feedback matters.
  4. H4: R-main approaches the oracle-replay ceiling — distillation loss is small.

3.6  Budget

~26 training runs × ≤1 h + replay evaluation ≈ 1–1.5 A100-days total. IsaacGym Preview 4 (CUDA-11 era) targets A100-class GPUs; H100 is not assumed to work.

5  Discussion & Limitations

  • Single hand so far. The modularity claim (one policy, N hands) needs Wuji/Sharpa, which requires authoring ManipTrans DexHand configs and re-running their transfer (~1–2 A100-days/hand for a task subset). Inspire-first is deliberate: it is the hand where kinematic retargeting is strongest, so H1 here is the conservative test.
  • Coupling conventions. Labels in the 6 independent joints sidestep the mimic-ratio disagreement, but deployment on hardware inherits the real mechanism's coupling; the residual channel is expected to absorb the millimetre-scale difference — unverified until rig experiments.
  • Composition with the base policy is out of scope here. This note evaluates R against recorded human streams; closed-loop composition with the frozen human policy πH is a separate workstream (see Base Policy).
  • Licensing. ManipTrans code/data are GPL-3.0 — fine for research use; code-release plans must account for it.
